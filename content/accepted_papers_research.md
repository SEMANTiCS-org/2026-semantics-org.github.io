<div class="accepted-title">Explaining RGNN Predictions on Knowledge Graphs via LRP</div>
<div class="accepted-subtitle"></div>
<p>Authors: Franz Krause, Heiko Paulheim</p>
<p>Relational Graph Neural Networks (RGNNs) have become powerful tools for learning on relational graph structures such as RDF knowledge graphs (KGs), achieving state-of-the-art performance in tasks such as node classification and link prediction. However, their black-box nature conflicts with a central objective of KGs: enabling transparent and interpretable knowledge-based systems. As a result, predictions produced by RGNNs are often difficult to explain in terms of the graph structure itself. In this work, we present a generalized framework for explaining RGNN predictions using Layer-Wise Relevance Propagation (LRP). The approach traces predictions through the relational message-passing process and redistributes signed relevance scores to nodes, edges, and multi-hop paths. In doing so, it establishes a semantic predictive loop in which predictions derived from the KG structure can themselves be explained with respect to the underlying computation graph. Beyond extending relevance propagation to relational message passing, the framework unifies structure-oriented and embedding-oriented explanations by linking graph components to embedding-level contributions. Experiments on standard KG learning benchmarks demonstrate that the proposed method yields faithful and interpretable explanations of RGNN predictions across multiple structural levels. </p>

<div class="accepted-title">SEOntology: A Domain Ontology for Semantic Modeling of Search Engine Optimization Workflows</div>
<div class="accepted-subtitle"></div>
<p>Authors: Emilija Gjorgjevska, David Riccitelli, Milos Jovanovik, Andrea Volpini</p>
<p>SEO workflows remain difficult to automate and integrate because they span heterogeneous tools while relying on loosely structured data models that provide limited machine-interpretable representations of content, actions, and performance signals. This fragmentation hinders interoperability and makes provenance tracking across SEO systems brittle. We present SEOntology, a Schema.org-aligned OWL 2 ontology that models SEO workflows as knowledge-graph structures to support structured querying and provenance-aware analysis. The ontology enables queries for content-quality inspection, entity-coverage analysis, and end-to-end tracing of analytical actions through their inputs, outputs, and generated signals. We assess SEOntology via structural ontology evaluation and competency-question validation, and report supporting evidence from production deployment. These results indicate logical consistency, coverage of the defined requirements, and practical utility as a semantic integration layer for representing and querying SEO workflows. SEOntology is designed as a reusable semantic resource for knowledge-graph–driven and agent-assisted SEO systems, and is extensible toward emerging generative and AI-mediated search environments.</p>

<div class="accepted-title">A Semantic Framework for the Synthesis of Causal Knowledge</div>
<div class="accepted-subtitle"></div>
<p>Authors: Katrin Ehrenmüller, Lucas Kook, Fajar J. Ekaputra, Marta Sabou</p>
<p>MOTIVATION: AI systems relying solely on correlations often fail when training and production distributions differ, limiting their utility for reliable decision-making. While incorporating causal knowledge offers a more robust alternative, synthesizing such knowledge from heterogeneous sources remains a fragmented and cognitively demanding task. There is currently a lack of support for integrating, comparing, and resolving discrepancies between multiple, often conflicting, causal graphs.  OBJECTIVES: We present SoCK (Synthesis of Causal Knowledge), a semantic framework designed to integrate, analyse, and explore causal graphs derived from heterogeneous sources. Our goal is to provide a unified representation of causal knowledge that supports expert-in-the-loop exploration and transforms differing causal graphs into a queryable knowledge base. The framework aims to provide an accessible interface for multiple stakeholders, focusing on knowledge engineers, causal discovery researchers, and domain experts.  METHODS: Following a requirements-driven design, we developed three core contributions: (i) the SoCK ontology provides a unified formalization of causal graphs and their provenance; (ii) the Causal Knowledge Graph integrates multi-source inputs into a unified knowledge base; and (iii) an Exploration Interface operationalizes complex semantic queries through task-based interactions.  RESULTS: We evaluate SoCK using the CausalAssembly benchmark dataset. Results demonstrate that the ontology provides high representational coverage, successfully answering 14 out of 15 competency questions. Furthermore, scenario-based evaluations show that the framework effectively supports stakeholder tasks, such as algorithm selection and structural discrepancy analysis, by enabling systematic comparison and exploration of causal graphs through the interface.  CONCLUSION: SoCK addresses a critical challenge in causal discovery by providing the first semantic infrastructure for managing the heterogeneity and ambiguity inherent in multi-source causal discovery. By integrating fragmented causal knowledge and providing an interactive exploration interface to query complex metadata, the framework offers a systematic foundation for comparing causal models and resolving structural causal discrepancies.</p>

<div class="accepted-title">Declaratively Populating Wikibases with WBML</div>
<div class="accepted-subtitle"></div>
<p>Authors: Élena Liu, Jakub Duchateau, Christophe Debruyne</p>
<p>Purpose: This paper addresses the lack of a general declarative approach for generating Wikibase content from heterogeneous data sources. Existing declarative mapping approaches mainly target RDF, while Wikibase-oriented solutions are often limited in scope or procedural in nature. We therefore propose WBML, a declarative mapping language tailored to the Wikibase statement model.  Methodology: WBML was designed by reusing selected RML concepts for logical sources and iterations, while introducing Wikibase-specific constructs. We implemented a Python prototype that translates WBML into intermediate RML and RDF representations, executes them with Morph-KGC, and ingests the results into Wikibase.  Findings: The study shows that declaratively generating Wikibase statements from heterogeneous sources is feasible. We also reported on a demonstration using complex JSON and a simpler, real-world use case for our institution, using CSV files. WBML supports the generation of entities, fingerprints, statements, qualifiers, references, and ranks.  Value: This work provides a basis for declarative construction of Wikibase knowledge graphs from heterogeneous sources. It demonstrates how established ideas from the RDF mapping community can be adapted to other types of knowledge graphs. Future work includes supporting newer RML features not yet supported by reused components and managing updates. </p>

<div class="accepted-title">A Knowledge Graph–Based Framework for Harmonising and Comparing Social Surveys</div>
<div class="accepted-subtitle"></div>
<p>Authors: Sara Zuppiroli, Silvia Cappa, Andrea Giovanni Nuzzolese</p>
<p>A wide range of tools in the social sciences, stemming from numerous studies in economics, political science, and sociology, requires systematic methods for reuse, integration, and international compari- son. To address this challenge, we present an integrated software archi- tecture that combines ontological modelling and knowledge-extraction techniques to detect, import, and reuse questionnaires in a simple and intuitive way. The system integrates a user-friendly web interface, a ques- tionnaire import module, and a semantic knowledge graph that formal- izes and interlinks core survey entities—such as questions, variables, and groups. This infrastructure supports both the construction of new ques- tionnaires and meaningful comparison across heterogeneous studies and diverse contexts. Built upon semantic technologies and established ontol- ogy design patterns, the architecture ensures an interoperable represen- tation of survey components and their relationships to social variables. Overall, the proposed system addresses a critical limitation of current research infrastructures in the social sciences: the difficulty of designing FAIR-by-design survey instruments capable of identifying, operationalis- ing, and systematically comparing relevant social variables across coun- tries and over time. </p>

<div class="accepted-title">OntoExtend: A Framework for Requirement-driven and Scalable Ontology Extension with LLMs</div>
<div class="accepted-subtitle"></div>
<p>Authors: Anna Sofia Lippolis, Javad Saeedizade, Eva Blomqvist, Aldo Gangemi, Andrea Giovanni Nuzzolese, Robin Keskisärkkä, Stefan Schmid, Simon Blattner</p>
<p>Ontology extension refers to the process of enriching an existing ontology in response to emerging requirements, making it more complete. This task is a resource-intensive and error-prone process. Large Language Models (LLMs) have shown promising performance on generating ontologies from scratch, but current approaches rarely tie ontology extension explicitly to requirements or reusable core models, and offer limited, systematic evaluation of LLM outputs. This paper intro- duces OntoExtend, a requirements-driven framework for ontology extension with LLMs. It uses retrieval-augmented generation (RAG) over relevant input ontologies and requirements in the form of competency questions to propose grounded extensions. To evaluate OntoExtend, we propose a multidimensional evaluation, including structural and functional assessment, as well as six ontology engineers who evaluate the generated extensions. The framework is instantiated and assessed on two use cases: a publicly available EU-project ontology and an industrial ontology. The results indicate little to no structural and logical issues and ontology engineers with high Fleiss inter-annotator agreement (Po) reported the generated fragments as semantically and syntactically correct, needing minor and moderate changes before being integrated into the input ontologies. These results suggest that the OntoExtend framework can support effective ontology extension in real-world scenarios. </p>

<div class="accepted-title">From File Systems to Knowledge Graphs: A Multi-Phase Workflow for the Description of Born-Digital Personal Archives</div>
<div class="accepted-subtitle"></div>
<p>Authors: Lucia Giagnolini, Paolo Bonora</p>
<p>Background: Born-digital personal archives present novel descriptive challenges that conventional archival methods are not equipped to address. Their scale, format heterogeneity, and multi-layered stratification — physical, logical, and conceptual — require a representational framework capable of capturing doc- ument structure, provenance, integrity, and relational contexts. The increasing vol- ume of born-digital archives received by cultural heritage institutions makes the development of automated and scalable description methodologies an urgent prac- tical priority. Methods: This paper presents a replicable five-phase pipeline, grounded in the Born-Digital Ontology (BoDi) — a formal extension of Records in Contexts Ontol- ogy (RiC-O) integrating PREMIS, PROV-O, and LRMoo — that transforms born- digital personal archive directories into RDF knowledge bases. The phases are: (1) file system ingestion, comprising SHA-256 integrity verification, parallel multi- tool metadata extraction, and triplestore loading; (2) SPARQL-based validation and rule-based enrichment, including cross-tool metadata alignment, document classi- fication, and duplicate detection; (3) hybrid enrichment combining expert biblio- graphic curation with automated LLM-based description generation; (4) chain-of- custody reconstruction; and (5) selective entity-level redaction for privacy-aware dissemination. Results: Evaluated on the Valerio Evangelisti Archive — 78,211 files and 11,135 folders totaling 2.1 TB across three storage media — the pipeline generated 61,154,322 RDF triples across 13 named graphs with minimal manual interven- tion. The resulting knowledge base supports querying across structural, typologi- cal, temporal, and relational dimensions, enabling analyses — from duplicate de- tection across storage media to correlation of archival activity with editorial output — that conventional finding aids cannot support. Conclusions: Born-digital archives present a double challenge: their fragility raises preservation concerns, while their scale renders manual description effec- tively impossible. The pipeline addresses both by inverting the framing: the prop- erties that make born-digital materials resistant to conventional methods become, within a formal framework, sources of descriptive richness without equivalent in the analog domain. Ontological modeling combined with automated processing and human-in-the-loop validation offers a viable and empirically demonstrated path to- ward semantic description of born-digital cultural heritage at scale.</p>

<div class="accepted-title">When Knowledge Graph Relationships Still Matter</div>
<div class="accepted-subtitle"></div>
<p>Authors: Alex Romanova</p>
<p>Modern language models increasingly recover signals of relatedness from text alone. They can support related-item ranking, entity clustering, and recommendation-style workflows without a separately modeled graph. For the knowledge graph community, this creates a timely and practical question: when do explicit graph relationships still add value beyond what strong text embeddings already capture?  This paper approaches that question carefully. It does not treat knowledge graphs as globally replaceable. Many KG roles — including semantic integration, provenance, governance, validation, querying, and domain modeling — remain distinct from embedding-based retrieval. Instead, we focus on a narrower class of use cases where relationship value is representational: recommendation, discovery, similarity search, related-item ranking, and relation recovery. In these settings, the question becomes measurable: do explicit relationships add recoverable signal beyond node text?  We introduce a relation-dependent diagnostic framework for measuring this boundary. We call the underlying phenomenon language-model encroachment: the growing ability of text-based representations to approximate relational signal traditionally supplied by explicit graph structure. Using an OpenAlex-derived scientific KG case study, we repeat the diagnostic across relation types, dataset slices, publication years, and text encoders. In each setting, text-derived and graph-derived representations are evaluated on the same held-out relationship recovery task. The results show that the graph–text boundary is not fixed. Some relation types are largely recoverable from text for this task. Others retain distinctive graph-derived signal.  The contribution is a practical diagnostic for KG engineering: if a relationship type adds signal beyond text, it may justify graph-engineering effort; if it does not add measurable signal, a text-first system may be sufficient for that task. This does not mean a KG is unnecessary overall, because many KG roles remain justified independently of embedding performance. </p>

<div class="accepted-title">Synthology: Ontology-Based Data Generation for Neurosymbolic Reasoning</div>
<div class="accepted-subtitle"></div>
<p>Authors: Vincent Van Schependom, Cas Proost, Pieter Bonte</p>
<p>Symbolic reasoners offer a simple contract: provide an ontology, receive entailments. Neurosymbolic reasoners promise the same task with added scalability and tolerance to noise, but break this contract by requiring an ontology-specific training dataset, which in most cases does not exist and must be gathered from scratch. This bottleneck blocks practitioners from applying neurosymbolic reasoners to new ontologies. We present Synthology, an ontology-agnostic synthetic data generator that closes this gap by producing the required training data from any OWL 2 RL ontology, with no need for manual data gathering. Synthology engineers data through ontology-guided backward-chaining proof search, which guarantees deep multi-hop derivations by construction, and introduces proof-based negative sampling. Together, these properties force models to traverse the full reasoning chain to correctly classify triples.     Our evaluation shows that a model trained on Synthology     data outperforms models trained on baseline data, with     a particularly robust advantage in rejecting near-miss     negatives. Proof-based negative sampling yields     consistent gains over random and constrained sampling     on all learning-quality metrics, and Synthology scales     to ontologies on which traditional (ontology specific)     generators run out of memory. To our knowledge,     Synthology is the first ontology-agnostic synthetic     data generator that provides multi-hop coverage,     near-miss negatives, and derivation metadata by     construction, a step toward making neurosymbolic     reasoners as readily deployable on a new ontology as     their symbolic counterparts.</p>

<div class="accepted-title">Surprising Effectiveness of Self-Demonstrations in Enhancing Schema-Ontology Mapping with LLMs</div>
<div class="accepted-subtitle"></div>
<p>Authors: Siddhesh Thombre, Manasi Patwardhan, Sunita Sarawagi</p>
<p>Integrating heterogeneous relational databases into a centralized ontology remains a persistent challenge in enterprise knowledge representation, primarily due to semantic heterogeneity, cryptic schema naming, missing metadata, and the abstraction gap between relational schemas and ontological models. Although large language models (LLMs) offer strong semantic reasoning capabilities, we show that directly applying them through one-shot prompting or naïve multi-stage pipelines leads to poor performance for schema–ontology mapping. This paper presents a self-demonstration-driven approach that combines a neuro-symbolic task decomposition with a novel mechanism for automatically generating pattern-guided, dependency-aware demonstrations to address this integration challenge. Our approach incorporates two key strategies to achieve substantial accuracy gains over existing LLM-based schema integration methods: (i) a neuro-symbolic decomposition of the task into cascaded sub-tasks, where symbolic constraints structure the search space and LLMs perform semantic reasoning within each focused sub-task, and (ii) self-generated demonstrations guided by domain-agnostic patterns  to supervise each sub-task. Experiments on three of the most challenging scenarios from the RODI benchmark show that our approach achieves state-of-the-art performance, substantially outperforming (~25 percentage points F1 improvements) both traditional schema-to-ontology mapping techniques and recent LLM-based schema-to-ontology and schema matching approaches. Ablation studies further reveal the significant benefits of pattern-guided self-demonstrations and the complementary benefits of neuro-symbolic task decomposition.</p>

<div class="accepted-title">Knowledge-driven Data Exchange and Validation Framework for Networked Smart Buildings</div>
<div class="accepted-subtitle"></div>
<p>Authors: Xander Wilcke, Barry Nouwt, Sophie Lathouwers, Ronald Siebes</p>
<p>Building Automation and Management Systems offer centralized control and monitoring by integrating smart devices into the environment. Together, these provide building owners and stakeholders with data about energy consumption and other relevant characteristics which can, among others, be used for decision making and the automation of actions. However, due to the proliferation of different protocols and ecosystems, it often remains challenging to seamlessly aggregate and analyse the data streams provided by the connected devices. To overcome these problems, we introduce a knowledge-driven data exchange and validation framework, built upon Semantic Web technologies, that a) facilitates the transparent flow of graph data between smart devices, b) validates these data and generates reports on detected anomalous behaviour in real-time, and c) aggregates and displays the data and any derived insight on dashboards and other interactive web-accessible interfaces. Our framework consists of two core components: the Knowledge Engine, which acts as an information broker and translation layer between device-specific languages and RDF, and the Knowledge Validator, which continuously monitors these RDF streams and reports on deviations from nominal behaviour. We evaluate our framework via requirement-based testing and demonstrate its functionality in a real-world case study in the smart buildings domain, showing that it can facilitate seamless communication between devices of different make and model and that it can help building owners recognize abnormal device behaviour. </p>

<div class="accepted-title">KGXL-Query: Extending SPARQL with Semantic Search and LLM functionality for Question Answering over Heterogeneous Data</div>
<div class="accepted-subtitle"></div>
<p>Authors: Tilahun Abedissa Taffa, Patrick Westphal, Debayan Banerjee, Ricardo Usbeck</p>
<p>In this paper, we introduce KGXL-Query, a new SPARQL functionality extension that embeds LLMs (Large Language Models) and semantic search directly into SPARQL, enabling hybrid inference where symbolic and non-symbolic methods coexist within a single, executable, and explainable framework. The quest to answer complex questions, those requiring both structured facts and contextual evidence from unstructured data, has shown a gap in knowledge systems: the incompatibility between symbolic and semantic inference. Knowledge Graphs (KGs) support formal, logically grounded querying via structured languages such as SPARQL. Conversely, LLMs and semantic text search offer vast coverage of real-world knowledge via vector-based similarity metrics. However, beyond using KGs for refining text or LLM results, and complementing missing information in KGs via LLMs or text search, there is less emphasis on accessing LLMs \& semantic search functionality through SPARQL. Moreover, existing Question Answering (QA) methods on heterogeneous data sources use RAG, data unification, and decompose-and-aggregate methods. The unifying approaches suffer from irreversible information loss; decomposition-driven methods are prone to cascading errors; and RAG methods lack explainability and are limited to shallow textual integration. Therefore, KGXL-Query paves the way by transforming the question to leverage the model rather than altering the data to fit it. </p>

<div class="accepted-title">Ontology-Aware Prompting for Knowledge Graph Construction from Text</div>
<div class="accepted-subtitle"></div>
<p>Authors: Balram Tiwari, Italo Oliveira, Asep Fajar Firmansyah, Hamada M Zahera, Frank Hopfgartner, Axel-Cyrille Ngonga Ngomo</p>
<p>Constructing Knowledge Graphs (KGs) from unstructured text has traditionally relied on multi-stage pipelines combining Named Entity Recognition, Relation Extraction, and Entity Linking components. These architectures are costly to adapt to new domains and suffer from error propagation across stages. While Large Language Models (LLMs) enable end-to-end triple generation from text, their direct application introduces two key challenges: (1) hallucination, where generated facts are not supported by the input text, and (2) ontology misalignment, where extracted entities and relations violate the constraints of a target schema. To address these limitations, we propose an ontology-aware multi-prompt framework that integrates the target ontology into the prompting process and applies hierarchical verification after triple generation. Specifically, we combine Tree-of-Thoughts-style reasoning for structured exploration, schema-constrained OpenIE, and relaxed triple generation to produce a diverse set of candidate triples while maintaining ontological awareness. A rule-based three-filter filtering module then refines these candidates using cross-prompt agreement, evidence grounding, and surface-form alignment, without relying on external toolchains. Experiments on Text2KGBench, including DBpedia-WebNLG and Wikidata-TekGen, show improvements of up to 2.3 times over LLM-based baselines, together with hallucination rates below 0.20 in most domains and ontology conformance above 0.90 on most DBpedia-WebNLG domains. The framework is released as open-source and available at https://anonymous.4open.science/r/ontology-aware-kg-construction-2B0C.</p>

<div class="accepted-title">Best Practices in Graph Retrieval-Augmented Generation: A Systematic Evaluation</div>
<div class="accepted-subtitle"></div>
<p>Authors: Yan-Ying Liao, Diego Collarana, Christopher Ingo Pack, Alexander Graß, Andreas Both, Stefan Decker, Christian Beecks</p>
<p>Graph Retrieval-Augmented Generation (GraphRAG) enhances Large Language Models (LLMs) by integrating knowledge graphs for structured retrieval and reasoning. However, the design space of GraphRAG systems is vast, and systematic guidance on optimal configurations remains scarce. In this paper, we present a comprehensive empirical study identifying best practices across the three core stages of the GraphRAG pipeline: graph-based indexing, graph-guided retrieval, and graph-enhanced generation. Through extensive experiments on multiple datasets (MSMARCO, HotpotQA, and a domain-specific legal corpus), we evaluate key design decisions, including chunking strategies, embedding model selection, prompt engineering for triplet extraction, entity resolution, retriever architectures, traversal depths, reranking models, and graph representation formats for LLM prompts. Our results show that hierarchical chunking at 512 tokens, schema-based extraction prompts, a combined Hypothetical Question and Entities retriever with cross-encoder reranking, and code-like graph representations (GraphML) yield robust performance across domains. We apply the identified best practices to a real-world use case within EU banking regulations, constructing a knowledge graph from the Capital Requirements Regulation (CRR) and the Capital Requirements Directive (CRD IV). A comparative evaluation demonstrates that the optimised GraphRAG system consistently outperforms Naive RAG, HyDE RAG, and Hybrid RAG, achieving overall win rates of 60.6%, 58.0%, and 67.4%, respectively, as judged by an LLM evaluator across comprehensiveness, diversity, empowerment, and correctness metrics. Our findings provide actionable guidelines for deploying GraphRAG in domain-specific, privacy-sensitive settings using open-source models.</p>

<div class="accepted-title">Navigating Schema Drift: Orchestrating Agents and Schema Discovery for Robust, Domain-specific Knowledge Graph Question Answering</div>
<div class="accepted-subtitle"></div>
<p>Authors: Anton Diettrich, Dirk Friedenberger, Andreas Both</p>
<p>Knowledge Graph Question Answering (KGQA) over Large Language Models (LLMs) increasingly translates natural-language questions into SPARQL, yet most systems are statically optimized for a single benchmark and degrade abruptly under cross-domain transfer or schema evolution—a recurring obstacle for industrial Knowledge Graphs whose schemas continuously evolve.  We present a KG-agnostic Text2SPARQL architecture that operates without task-specific fine-tuning. A meta-controller orchestrates a ReAct agent and an execution-validated, schema-guided fallback pipeline; both paths are grounded by a runtime FAISS-indexed Schema-Discovery service that absorbs structural change without offline retraining. The system is evaluated across four heterogeneous KGs: the open-domain DBpedia, a structurally complex Corporate dataset, and two versions of a proprietary railway metadata KG (TrackVideo, German: Streckenvideo), whose v1→v2 transition exhibits a Mapping Ratio of 0.255 and a normalized graph edit distance of 0.750, representing radical schema refactoring.  The hybrid architecture attains a mean F1 of 47.6% with the lowest cross-domain standard deviation (2.33) among compared state-of-the-art systems and preserves performance between TrackVideo v1 (48.0%) and v2 (48.6%) despite the drift. A multi-model Quorum extension over heterogeneous LLM families further increases reliability, with absolute F1 gains of up to +7.0%. The findings support the HARP paradigm—hybrid agentic retrieval with execution-validated routing—for portable, schema-drift-resilient KGQA in production-grade settings.</p>

<div class="accepted-title">DQV4AI: Representing AI Quality Dimensions for ISO and EU AI Act using the Data Quality Vocabulary</div>
<div class="accepted-subtitle"></div>
<p>Authors: Delaram Golpayegani, Beyza Yaman, Fabian Linde, Riccardo Albertoni, Harshvardhan J. Pandit</p>
<p>As the use of AI proliferates rapidly, its development and deployment across all stages requires understanding and documentation regarding aspects such as its accuracy, fairness, and transparency---collectively called 'quality characteristics'. This has led to standardisation at forums such as ISO, and creation of obligations within regulations such as the EU's AI Act. To support this developing landscape, we assess the sufficiency of the Data Quality Vocabulary (DQV), which is a W3C Working Note, for representing AI quality dimensions. We find that while the DQV can be useful, some concepts require changes to the definition to enable use beyond datasets, for AI models and systems. Based on our findings, we propose updates to  DQV concepts to allow a more general quality representation and provide taxonomies based on ISO/IEC DIS 25059 standard on AI quality models and the EU AI Act to represent key AI quality dimensions and examples of their use. We conclude with a discussion on the future of AI quality documentation, the role of Semantic Web standards, and facilitating interoperability across value chains and data spaces.</p>
