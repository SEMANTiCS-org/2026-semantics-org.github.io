<div class="accepted-title">Comparing Knowledge Graph Use in Large Scale Technical Documentation Projects</div>
<div class="accepted-subtitle"></div>
<p>Authors: Martin Blumbach and Ulrike Parson</p>
<p>Technical product documentation helps customers use the full potential of complex products. However, as release cycles shorten, variants grow and reliable data for agentic AI is required, technical documentation faces rising demands. Knowledge graphs organize product information and connect data across enterprise silos, making them useful for technical documentation. They also offer more reliable ground truth for agentic AI than vector RAG-based document ingestion, which often has issues with multi-hop retrieval and inconsistent terminology. From an enterprise architecture point of view, a knowledge graph platform sits like a “spider in the web” between data sources and content delivery. We present and compare the use of knowledge graphs in different (non-joint) large-scale documentation projects. Both projects have knowledge graphs in productive use, and each project represents a difficult scenario for technical documentation:

* Endress+Hauser (represented by parson): Smaller, built-to-order products for process automation with a very high number of product variants 

* Ericsson: Larger software products for 5G core networks with a common, highly configurable platform The session will have the following structure: 

* Introduction to the projects 
    * Why use knowledge graphs 
    * Objectives of projects 
    * Scope of the solutions 

* System architecture and current implementation status 
    * Components 
    * W3C standards vs. proprietary solutions 
    * Semantic models for product structures and information types 
    * Automation of processes 
    * Integration with genAI 

* Future use 
    * Integration of chatbot for content delivery 
    * Extension of documentation solution to corporate semantic middleware and knowledge hub 
    * MCP server integration 
    * Use of AI agents 
    * Generate smart content from legacy content 

* Learnings 
    * How to manage the implementation project 
    * How to convince stakeholders 
    * Change management and changed ways of working; new roles for technical communicators

<div class="accepted-title">Using gUFO patterns to create ontologies: unraveling the nature of employment contracts</div>
<div class="accepted-subtitle"></div>
<p>Authors: Kees Nieuwstad and Han Joosten</p>
<p>The Dutch Police need a set of integrated domain ontologies to support their operations and information management. To achieve this goal, they initially adopted DOLCE (implemented in OWL) as an upper ontology to ground domain ontology development. However, DOLCE did not offer modeling patterns to ensure consistency and communication among modelers and stakeholders. The team ended up having different conventions and design decisions that harmed the goal achievement. To overcome these challenges, the police team with our team joined forces to adopt gUFO as the backbone of the ontology development for this project. gUFO is a lightweight OWL implementation of the Unified Foundational Ontology (UFO) that consists of several ontology design patterns that solve common modeling problems in ontology engineering. When our teams worked with these patterns, we noticed that we obtained better communication and higher-quality models. To streamline the application of gUFO patterns, we use OWL reasoning and implemented SPARQL queries that automatically validate ontology design choices continuously for all developers involved in the process. Therefore, we created a collaborative environment that ensures certain ontology quality metrics, following gUFO patterns. One of these patterns allows us to represent different types of employment contracts faithfully. For example, in gUFO, a relator is a relationship treated as a thing in its own right. Instead of just saying "John works for the Police", "John’s contract" is created as a concrete entity that can have its own properties like a start date or legal obligations. In this case, the relator connects an employee to the Police organization. We do not only adopt the pattern but also enforce it via an automated validation pipeline, quickly detecting errors.</p>

<div class="accepted-title">From MARC21 to WEMI Without a Library-Software Vendor: A Semantic-Platform Bet</div>
<div class="accepted-subtitle"></div>
<p>Authors: Jeroen Wouters</p>
<p>Cultuurconnect runs the central catalogue that 300 Flemish public libraries use to catalogue their holdings, with around 60 cataloguers working in it on a typical day. The data sits in MARC21, the legacy library exchange format, even though IFLA's WEMI/LRM model has been the agreed direction for years and most library-software vendors are still slow to ship against it. In 2024 Cultuurconnect concluded that waiting for the next vendor release was not going to get them where they needed to be, and looked across the office floor in Ghent, where Inuits had been building a generic semantic platform called Elody. A joint proof of concept that summer turned into a full build a year later; the new system goes into production in June 2026. Our pitch was contrarian: do not look for a library-software vendor that has yet to ship WEMI; pair a semantic-platform team with the customer's own domain expertise. The catalogue is now an entity-relation model with a thin collection service that distributes data to and from external sources (VIAF, Boekenbank, Muziekweb), with a real-time MARC21 stream kept alive for downstream consumers. The model holds roughly 3 million WEMI entities, is already published as Linked Open Data, and is shaped by cataloguers through ongoing sprint feedback — without compromising the WEMI model we were aiming for. The lesson we did not expect when we started: sector expertise is not the limiting factor in this kind of project. A serious technology partner paired with the customer's own domain people consistently beats a sector-specific software vendor that hasn't yet implemented the new standard.</p>

<div class="accepted-title">Smart Buildings, Smart Future: Semantic Data Integration for Smart Buildings including a Federated Data Model</div>
<div class="accepted-subtitle"></div>
<p>Authors: Apostolos Barekas, Pieter Pauwels and Stefan Hoekstra</p>
<p>Our buildings are getting more and more data-driven, thus providing the potential of making them smarter and ‘more intelligent’. However, 90% of available IoT and sensor data in building systems currently tends to go to waste, and remains unused. This operational data loss is caused by data fragmentation, the use of isolated and often proprietary closed systems, and the absence of standardized data exchange protocols, obstacles that consistently prevent building owners from deploying affordable, scalable solutions to create facilities that are more effectively used. To solve this problem, the Dutch public-private partnership initiative SuGeST (Smart Buildings, Smart Future) is establishing an open, standard-based digital backbone for commercial real estate. By leveraging a decentralized, federated data model and semantic middleware, SuGeST unifies highly diverse data sources, including Building Information Modeling (BIM), Building Management Systems (BMS), and time-series sensor data, while securely keeping all data at their original sources. Built on FAIR data principles and closely aligned with international ontologies, such as Brick, SAREF, and BOT, the project delivers practical data structuring guidelines alongside an independent data layer proof-of-concept. Integration is achieved using a system integration approach. The framework is validated across two smart living labs at the Technical Universities of Delft (The Green Village) and Eindhoven. By improving data access, the independent layer simplifies algorithm training, making it easier to deploy specific MPC hardware and software as a building's base control system based on its semantic parameters. The independent layer aims to demonstrate a 20-25% reduction in system implementation time alongside a 10–20% improvement in energy efficiency and indoor climate comfort. Ultimately, the project provides a definitive, actionable roadmap for real estate owners, installers, and software vendors to claim true data ownership at the source and accelerate the sustainable transformation of the built environment.</p>

<div class="accepted-title">Privacy-Preserving Sharing of Knowledge Graphs at the Bank of Italy</div>
<div class="accepted-subtitle"></div>
<p>Authors: Luigi Bellomarini, Costanza Catalano, Andrea Coletta and Michela Iezzi</p>
<p>Corporate ownership graphs are a strategic knowledge asset for banking supervision and financial intelligence, but their dissemination is constrained by strict confidentiality requirements. This talk presents an industrial case study from the Central Bank of Italy on privacy-preserving sharing of corporate ownership Knowledge Graphs (KGs). In our setting, the KG is not only an extensional graph of asserted ownership triples, but a semantic knowledge base whose intensional layer is specified by Datalog rules. These rules define entailment over the graph and derive supervisory notions such as corporate control, ultimate control, joint control, integrated ownership, and qualifying holdings. As a result, what must be protected is not only the explicit graph, but the combination of asserted data and rule-based inferences. This requirement is central in institutional data-sharing scenarios involving external analysts, researchers, and supervisory partners. Existing anonymization and graph-sanitization techniques typically focus on structural disclosure in the released graph, while they do not address confidential information that may be reconstructed through the semantic rule layer. This gap is relevant as KGs become core assets for analytics and grounding layers for LLM-based applications in high-confidentiality domains. We introduce a semantic anonymization framework for corporate ownership KGs. The method transforms the graph through controlled node and edge additions, entity relabeling, and weight perturbation, while taking the Datalog program into account to prevent both direct disclosure and inference-based leakage. We validate the framework on the Central Bank of Italy ownership dataset and on external benchmarks, assessing formal privacy protection, preservation of supervisory entailments, structural distortion, and downstream analytical utility. The experiments show that the anonymized KGs retain the semantic content required for regulatory analysis while preventing the disclosure of confidential information.</p>

<div class="accepted-title">AI-Driven Ontology Development for Multi-Lingual and Multi-Jurisdiction Financial Services - the HYPOPASS case</div>
<div class="accepted-subtitle"></div>
<p>Authors: Leon Roseleur</p>
<p>Creating a data standard across multiple jurisdictions isn't a technology problem. The real challenge lays in the fact that the same concept often has a different meaning depending on the legal system, language, or sector in which it is used. Standardizing on the technical layer does not solve those differences, unless organizations connect on both the semantic, the legal and the process-level. Otherwise, interoperability remains limited. We refer to this challenge as the legal-semantic gap. A good example is HypoPass, a Belgian-Dutch mortgage industry initiative that connects 16 banks and thousands of financial advisors across Belgium. The initiative spans three languages (Dutch, French and English), brings together 14 different data models and handles different jurisdictions across Wallonia, Flanders and Brussels. We were asked to develop interoperability between all parties involved. The complete data exchange consisted of approximately 4,800 definitions. Using conventional methods, this exercise was expected to take around 18 months. By applying an AI-supported methodology aligned with the European Interoperability Framework (EIF), we delivered a validated ontology within 13 weeks while maintaining an error rate below 0.5% - connecting the legal, semantic, organizational and layer. A key design principle is that experts remain in control throughout the process. AI performs the initial analysis and proposes mappings, while legal, business and domain specialists validate the results before moving to the next stage. This approach reduces the effort required per concept from hours to minutes and shortens overall delivery timelines tremendously. After this initial project, the same method tested and applied within various use cases spanning from Tax Office, Customs, Life Sciences and company law.</p>

<div class="accepted-title">GRMP: An open-source platform for automatic monitoring of linked data resources on the web</div>
<div class="accepted-subtitle"></div>
<p>Authors: Stijn Denis, Marc Portier, Cedric Decruw and Stijn Deknudt</p>
<p>When publishing linked data-related resources through the web, it is strongly advised to actively monitor them and ensure they remain accessible, up-to-date and continue to provide data in the intended manner. Public registries and dashboards do exist, but they require active maintenance themselves lest they become outdated and provide incorrect information regarding the status of these resources. In order to support the management of their own LDES-servers and SPARQL endpoints and monitor the status of external web resources they consume, the scientific institute VLIZ (Vlaams Instituut voor de Zee – Flanders Marine Institute) developed GRMP (Graph Resources Monitoring Platform) in cooperation with Flemish IT and Software company Sirus. GRMP is an open-source and highly modular platform which allows for the automatic monitoring of linked data resources and web resources in general. GRMP is container-based and primarily consists of an orchestrator which manages and directs test suite containers based on configurations defined in one or more YAML-files. Due to its modular nature, users can rapidly develop new test suites in any desired programming language provided that they have been containerized and adhere to a few guidelines allowing for proper communication with the orchestrator. Additionally, the environment on top of which the system runs can be freely chosen, ranging from local deployments to integration with automation workflows like GitHub Actions. When running within GitHub Actions, additional features are available such as automatic issue creation resulting from failed tests. This talk by Stijn Denis from Sirus will provide an overview and demonstration of the current VLIZ GRMP implementation, highlighting how it can be rapidly customized and/or expanded upon for use by any organization to automatically run regularly scheduled test suites and monitor resources on the web.</p>

<div class="accepted-title">From Spreadsheets to Shared Understanding: Navigating Water Catchment Data with a Composable Linked Data Architecture</div>
<div class="accepted-subtitle"></div>
<p>Authors: Andrew Fergusson and Chris Jarvis</p>
<p>Environmental regulators hold the data that citizens, consultants, planners, and engineers need to understand their local water catchments — discharge permits, monitoring records, land-use classifications, and water quality measurements. Yet broad technical and domain expertise is currently required to identify and use the right datasets, creating a significant FAIR data gap that limits the social, economic, and environmental value that this data could otherwise deliver. The Environment Agency (EA) and Telespazio have addressed this through a composable Linked Data architecture built on the DEFRA Data Services Platform Application Profile — a federation standard aligned to W3C SKOS, DCAT, SOSA/SSN, OWL, and OGC GeoSPARQL. The platform enables domain teams to publish standards-compliant Linked Data APIs from existing data sources, without requiring schema harmonisation or bespoke integration code. Domain systems retain their own vocabularies and express — once — how their terms relate to the shared profile; the federated layer handles the rest. The Water Catchment Demonstrator presents a live user journey grounded in EA's open data. Starting from any location in the United Kingdom, the system identifies the relevant river catchment and traverses the federated data layer to surface discharge permits, compliance monitoring returns, water quality observations, and land-use classifications — without the user needing to know where any dataset lives or how to interpret its observational data. The demonstrator evaluates the Application Profile and technical approaches as a practical federation standard for DEFRA and EA's open data, and provides evidence that composable Linked Data architecture can meaningfully reduce the expertise barrier to accessing and using complex regulatory datasets.</p>

<div class="accepted-title">ISGBot: An Agentic Multimodal Knowledge Framework for Semantic Access to the Interinstitutional Style Guide</div>
<div class="accepted-subtitle"></div>
<p>Authors: Giorgos Filandrianos, Vassilis Tzouvaras, Giorgos Stamou, Nathalie Foigne, Denis Dechandon and Aniko Gerencser</p>
<p>The Interinstitutional Style Guide (ISG) is the primary reference for drafting, editing, translating and publishing documents across the European Union institutions. While the guide contains a large body of editorial, linguistic, legal and publication knowledge, its size and complexity can make the retrieval of relevant information challenging, particularly for users unfamiliar with its structure. To address this challenge, we developed ISGBot, an agentic knowledge framework that combines multimodal retrieval, semantic technologies, reference data and Large Language Models to provide conversational access to the content of the ISG. ISGBot integrates information from PDF documents, extracted text, tables, images, captions, metadata and structured semantic resources represented in RDF and SKOS. A multimodal semantic representation layer combines text embeddings, image embeddings and semantic relations, while concepts identified within the content are linked to authoritative reference data and controlled vocabularies from EU Vocabularies. The retrieval architecture combines BM25, dense vector retrieval using Sentence-Transformers and FAISS, cross-encoder reranking, and RDF/SKOS graph navigation. Unlike traditional Retrieval-Augmented Generation approaches, ISGBot employs an agentic retrieval loop in which Large Language Models assess context sufficiency, reformulate queries, consolidate evidence and generate grounded responses. The prototype was developed and validated in close collaboration with the ISG team of the Publications Office of the European Union, whose feedback was used to evaluate and refine the system. The resulting framework demonstrates how multimodal retrieval, semantic technologies and agentic AI can be combined to provide evidence-based access to complex organisational knowledge resources.</p>

<div class="accepted-title">POPULATING AND EVALUATING A KNOWLEDGE GRAPH FOR RISK MANAGEMENT IN MEDICAL DEVICES</div>
<div class="accepted-subtitle"></div>
<p>Authors: Guilherme Semedo, Rodrigo Carvalho, Emanuel Valpaços, Liliana Teles, Mariana Pinto, Edmundo Miranda, Pedro Oliveira, Pedro Colarejo and Bruno Gago</p>
<p>Manufacturers of medical devices are required to systematically manage risks under ISO 14971, a process that remains largely manual and dependent on domain expertise. Large Language Models offer interesting automation potential, but their adoption in regulated contexts requires traceable outputs, which is something that isolated LLM pipelines do not guarantee. This use case describes a methodology for grounding LLM risk management in a Knowledge Graph populated from real-world incident data, and introduces a consensus-based framework for evaluating the quality of the AI pipeline before deployment. We collected incident reports from the FDA MAUDE database across 26 product codes, yielding approximately 28,000 reports. A deduplication and normalization pipeline combining narrative normalization, text embeddings, and clustering reduced the results to a representative set of incidents per product family. For each incident cluster, we generated reasonably foreseeable sequences of events drawing on normalized narratives, patient problem codes, and device characteristics extracted from regulatory submission documents. Regulatory affairs specialists curated the outputs. Because no single ground-truth risk matrix exists under ISO 14971, we developed a structured expert consensus methodology. Panelists assessed baseline risk matrices, then reconciled them. Their agreed outputs form a gold standard against which further AI-generated risk matrices are benchmarked using a composite score. The methodology is replicable, device-agnostic, and designed to be integrated into electronic Quality Management Systems. Each ingestion cycle strengthens the Knowledge Graph, improving the quality of subsequent generation. To our knowledge, no published approach covers automated ISO 14971 compliant risk matrix generation with this combination of incident grounding, human curation, and continuous quality benchmarking.</p>

<p>Healthcare information such as medications and vaccinations is typically recorded using national product names, local terminologies, and language-specific descriptions. While this information is meaningful within a country, it often becomes difficult to interpret across borders. Citizens travelling abroad and healthcare professionals treating international patients may struggle to identify equivalent medications, understand vaccination histories, or communicate treatment information effectively. This presentation introduces a semantic approach developed by FAQIR Institute to improve the cross-border understanding and exchange of medication and vaccination information. The approach uses internationally recognized standards and ontologies, including SNOMED CT, IDMP, the IDMP Ontology (IDMP-O), the ATC Classification System, and EMA SPOR controlled vocabularies. Vaccinations are linked to SNOMED CT concepts to identify vaccine types independently of national product names, while medications are represented through their active substances, pharmaceutical forms, strengths, and regulatory characteristics. FAQIR Institute maintains semantic representations of medicinal products from multiple countries. By combining semantic annotations with international classifications, the platform can identify equivalent or closely matching medicinal products and vaccine types across countries and languages. The hierarchical structures of SNOMED CT and ATC also enable semantic browsing, allowing users to discover broader or related therapeutic and vaccine categories. The use case demonstrates how semantic technologies can support cross-border healthcare, improve patient safety, enable language-independent understanding, and promote interoperability through the reuse of internationally recognized standards. The work is currently being integrated into the Personal Health Record Ontology (PHR-O) and implemented in Qwenda, a mobile application based on person-centric data vaults. Together, these initiatives illustrate how semantic technologies can transform national health information into interoperable, multilingual, and reusable knowledge that supports future digital health services.</p>

<div class="accepted-title">Automated legal reasoning system for generating draft responses to claims, based on Neurosymbolic AI on knowledge graphs.</div>
<div class="accepted-subtitle"></div>
<p>Authors: Ricardo Alonso-Maturana, Angel Esteban Moreno-Beltrán, Marcos Campazas, Susana López-Sola and Karmele Alonso Aia</p>
<p>If this case is selected, the final case presentation is subject to approval by our client. An insurance entity manages thousands of lawsuits annually across two highly complex regulatory domains: extraordinary risks (floods, earthquakes, terrorist attacks) and compulsory motor vehicle insurance. Each case file arrives as heterogeneous documentary chaos: procedural briefs, expert reports, photographs, scanned administrative files. Processing it through traditional methods represents weeks of specialized legal work, with the risk of argumentative inconsistency and exposure to deadlines that the judicial system does not forgive. GNOSS has built and deployed an automated legal reasoning system that does not merely retrieve relevant documents, but reasons over them. Its core is a knowledge graph encoding the regulatory pyramid applicable to the fund — from the Constitution and EU law down to administrative circulars — integrated with a jurisprudential layer of five thousand CENDOJ rulings. The system extracts the legally material entities from the case file, classifies the case, traverses the corresponding argumentative chain, and identifies the optimal procedural strategy from twelve predefined options. Each inference is an examinable logical instance, not a statistical prediction. The result is a tool that complies by design with the explainability and auditability requirements that the European AI Act demands of high-risk systems. The lawyer does not receive an answer: they receive a line of reasoning they can follow, challenge, and sign. What previously required days of work is completed in minutes, with full traceability and systematic argumentative consistency.</p>
