# Accepted Papers (Blue Sky Track)

## Finalists

<div class="accepted-item">
<div class="accepted-title">From Sequential Language to Structural Intelligence: Toward Relationship-Aware AI</div>
<div class="accepted-subtitle"></div>
<p>Authors: Alex Romanova</p>
<p>Large language models have transformed artificial intelligence by showing how much can be learned from language at scale. Their success has made language appear to be the dominant substrate of intelligence. This Blue Sky paper argues that this view is incomplete. We focus on one specific gap: what may be missing when relationships and structure are learned primarily through language.  We argue that LLMs face two language bottlenecks. First, they are token-sequence systems: even with attention and rich internal representations, they process and generate language through sequences of tokens. Second, their training data is human language, which is the sequential expression of cognition rather than the full relational process from which cognition emerges. Neuroscience gives reason to take this distinction seriously: the connectome and network neuroscience describe the brain in terms of elements, connections, and graph-like organization, while recent work argues that language is primarily a tool for communication rather than thought itself.  This suggests that LLMs learn from linguistic traces of intelligence, but not necessarily from the full relational processes that produce those traces. We therefore argue for relationship-aware AI as an open research direction: not a solved architecture, but a search for ways to combine linguistic fluency with explicit awareness of relationships and structure. The central distinction is between inference-time use of relationships and training-time learning from relationships. Many current systems add structure after a language model has already been trained, through retrieval, graph augmentation, tools, memory, or verification layers. These approaches are important, but the deeper Blue Sky challenge is to make relationships part of representation learning itself, so that structural awareness is learned by the model rather than supplied only after training.</p>
</div>

<div class="accepted-item">
<div class="accepted-title">From Fact to Feeling: Narrative Knowledge Graphs for Human-AI Interaction</div>
<div class="accepted-subtitle"></div>
<p>Authors: Anna Sofia Lippolis and Luiz Do Valle Miranda</p>
<p>Knowledge Graphs (KGs) and Large Language Models (LLMs) each capture a different dimension of human knowledge, and neither captures both. KGs excel at representing factual, structured information, yet remain blind to the perspectival and narrative texture through which humans construct and communicate meaning. LLMs absorb this texture implicitly from language, yet do so without explicit semantic structure, leaving their interpretive operations opaque, difficult to revise, and impossible to hold accountable. The next major challenge for Semantic Web research is developing formal infrastructures for the knowledge that falls between these two: perspectival, narrative, and metaphorically organized. We propose Narrative Knowledge Graphs (NKGs) as such an infrastructure. Grounded in computational hermeneutics, NKGs extend standard RDF foundations along three axes: perspective-annotated named graphs anchoring assertions to situated standpoints; narrative ontologies representing how the same events yield different accounts from different positions; and metaphorical frames, grounded in frame semantics, making explicit which conceptual structure a community or individual projects onto a domain. Together, these axes define a semantic layer designed to mediate between human experience and LLM-generated meaning. We position NKGs as a new fundamental layer for human-AI interaction, aimed at making machine-supported interpretation more explicit, revisable, and faithful to human meaning-making.</p>
</div>

<div class="accepted-item">
<div class="accepted-title">Can a Knowledge Graph Fall Down? Embodied Knowledge Graphs for Humanoid Robots</div>
<div class="accepted-subtitle"></div>
<p>Authors: Blerina Spahiu and Loris Roveda</p>
<p>Knowledge graphs (KGs) have become powerful instruments for representing, integrating, and querying knowledge. Humanoid robots expose a fundamental limitation of current world models: they can predict future states, but they rarely make explicit the semantic commitments needed to decide whether an object is reachable, a surface is supportive, a human is too close, or an action is unsafe. This paper argues that the next long-term challenge for Semantic Web and Knowledge Graph research is to build <i>Embodied Knowledge Graphs</i>: dynamic, multimodal, uncertainty-aware semantic world models whose statements are grounded in geometry, perception, proprioception, affordances, physical constraints, and predicted action consequences. Such graphs would not merely answer ``what is true?&#x27;&#x27; but also make explicit which commitments are grounded enough to act upon, which remain risky, and which require further perception. This is not a proposal for adding a knowledge graph to a robot; it is a proposal for redefining what a knowledge graph must be when its statements can trigger physical action. Using humanoid semantic world reconstruction as a motivating stress test, the paper outlines a research agenda around joint geometric-semantic reconstruction, structural abstraction, affordance transfer, provenance, and self-maintenance. The proposal is intentionally high-risk: it asks whether knowledge graphs must become useful not only by representing the world, but by making embodied action safer, more recoverable, and more auditable than systems based only on latent world models or scene graphs.</p>
</div>

<div class="accepted-item">
<div class="accepted-title">From Server-centric to Client-centric Data Integration over Decentralized Knowledge Graphs with Personal Query Engines</div>
<div class="accepted-subtitle"></div>
<p>Authors: Ruben Taelman</p>
<p>Knowledge Graph technologies provide us with the ability to interlink data across organizational boundaries, which lead to implicit Decentralized Knowledge Graphs (DKGs). This interlinking ability makes DKGs a natural foundation for public and private data, as all data can be stored and managed by the people or authorities owning this data. When one wants to build applications or services on top of DKGs, this decentralized nature leads to major technical challenges regarding data integration. Today, most of these challenges are being tackled in a server-centric manner, where decentralized data is integrated and centralized within servers that run somewhere in the cloud, far from the control of the people that actually own the data. Consequentially, this server-centricity lies at the root of many privacy concerns surrounding personal data. In this article, I argue that the ability to store data in a decentralized manner is insufficient for solving issues surrounding privacy and trustworthiness. Additionally, we need rethink how and where we integrate this data, and perform computations over it. Concretely, this article focuses on a paradigm shift from server-centric to client-centric data integration, by providing users with reusable and personal query engines. These are client-side engines that are in the driving seat for integrating decentralized data, as a basis for offering users with full algorithmic control over how their data is processed, in a transparent manner, which is private by design. This article discusses three open research challenges that are fundamental to client-centric query processing for driving data integration within decentralized applications. Namely, the challenges of heterogeneity, personalization, and performance. While server-centric data integration maximizes performance by sacrificing user control, transparency, and privacy, a client-centric approach not require this sacrifice, by shifting focus to the client. This offers a new paradigm for application domains where decentralization is an inherent property, such as healthcare, social media, and personal genomics.</p>
</div>

<div class="accepted-item">
<div class="accepted-title">PROV-CRED: Rated Provenance for Knowledge Graphs</div>
<div class="accepted-subtitle"></div>
<p>Authors: Mary Ann Tan, Christian Nennemann, Genet Asefa Gesese and Harald Sack</p>
<p>Knowledge graphs treat triples derived from deterministic schema rules, heuristic matching, and model prediction as equivalent in reliability and credibility, though their accuracy and failure modes differ. A hallucinated relation or mislinked entity deposited alongside schema-mapped facts enters downstream retrieval without distinction; errors recirculate as authoritative ground truth, compounding with each pass. PROV-O records that derivation occurred; enrichment method, producing model, and benchmark performance are absent from the graph, including for agentic systems that maintain graph-backed memory. PROV-CRED is a minimal PROV-O extension: enrichment method, model benchmark performance, and institutional accreditation are recorded as queryable PROV-O properties of each triple. Calibration, granularity, and retroactive provenance remain open problems</p>
</div>

<div class="accepted-item">
<div class="accepted-title">Knowledge Tectonics: A Geodynamic Inspired Framework for Modeling Epistemic Changes</div>
<div class="accepted-subtitle"></div>
<p>Authors: Thomas Van Erven, Stelios Kontogiannis, Efstratios Kontopoulos, Konstantinos Avgerinakis, Andrea Scharnhorst and Sándor Darányi</p>
<p>Emerging knowledge can be envisioned as protruding magma. This paper describes how such a metaphoric view can be turned into a model to capture the “continental drift” of concepts in an epistemic lithosphere. We call this new approach Knowledge Tectonics. We detail conceptual, mathematical and engineering operations to create such a scalable framework which allows us to interpret and manage knowledge evolution within Semantic Web environments. We use the WikiArt Emotions dataset which contains information on 4,105 paintings spanning 600 years to construct a proof-of-concept interface which enables visual analytics of where artworks are situated in a specific landscape of features. We demonstrate how fused semantic and pragmatic metadata can be modelled as evolving pressure zones. This way we are making “forces” behind the evolution of artifacts of creativity visible. Core elements of our new workflow are gradient vector fields derived from Poisson potential surfaces applied onto dynamic knowledge graphs, eventually capturing stylistic and emotional shifts as directed intensity flows. By treating artefacts of creative processes as a dynamic manifold, we provide a novel methodology for quantifying the “drift” of human inquiry. We argue that this approach is applicable also to other areas of creative human actions, including scientific knowledge production.</p>
</div>

<div class="accepted-item">
<div class="accepted-title">Semantics for Science: Toward a Massive Scale Evidence-Centric Scientific Knowledge Graph</div>
<div class="accepted-subtitle"></div>
<p>Authors: Mayank Kejriwal</p>
<p>Scientific knowledge is still communicated primarily through papers, while the actual objects that science accumulates are more granular: claims, methods, datasets, experimental traces, replications, contradictions, and theories. This mismatch has become more severe as scientific literature grows rapidly and large language models (LLMs) make the production and consumption of scientific text easier but not necessarily more reliable. In this blue-sky paper, we argue that semantic technologies should treat scientific assertions as first-class objects and support the creation of evidence-centric scientific knowledge graphs (KGs) directly from literature. We call this vision <i>semantics for science</i>. We argue that building such KGs is not simply a larger information extraction problem. It requires explicit representations of complex evidence traces, including provenance, uncertainty, scope conditions, temporal evolution, evidential support, and challenge relations. We further argue that the resulting infrastructure may require a reasoning engine for science that goes beyond conventional description logics entailment or graph traversal, combining symbolic semantics with defeasible, provenance-aware, and evidence-weighted reasoning. We outline a research agenda spanning representation languages, extraction pipelines, identity and alignment, contradiction handling, temporal revision, and benchmark design. Our claim is deliberately ambitious: if the Semantic Web community wants to shape how scientific knowledge is represented in the age of language models and AI agents, it should help move science from paper-centric publication systems toward computable, evidence-aware knowledge substrates.</p>
</div>

<div class="accepted-item">
<div class="accepted-title">The Semantic Immune System: Adaptive Knowledge Integrity in the Era of Induced Semantics</div>
<div class="accepted-subtitle"></div>
<p>Authors: Manzoor Ali, Yasir Mahmood, Muhammad Saleem and Axel-Cyrille Ngonga Ngomo</p>
<p>Knowledge graphs are increasingly populated by large language models (LLMs), which generate induced semantics at scale. However, LLM-generated knowledge also introduces hallucinations, contradictions, and semantic drift that static validation approaches such as SHACL and OWL cannot adequately address. We propose the Semantic Immune System (SIS), a logic-based framework for autonomous knowledge graph validation and repair. Inspired by biological immune systems, SIS combines deterministic constraints with adaptive and evolutionary mechanisms to detect, remember, and mitigate semantic anomalies in dynamically evolving graphs. We provide a formalization, a worked example, and a discussion of feasibility and oracle assumptions. The framework offers a blueprint for building autonomous KG repair systems that balance correctness, robustness, and completeness.</p>
</div>
